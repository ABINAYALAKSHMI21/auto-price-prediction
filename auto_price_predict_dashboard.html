PROBLEM STATEMENT

Why price prediction, and why it matters to a dealership

The dataset carries 26 attributes per vehicle — engine layout, body style, drivetrain, fuel system, dimensions, and an insurance risk rating — for 200 cars imported in 1985. The goal is a model that estimates price directly from those specs, so manufacturers can see how design choices move price, and dealers can sanity-check listings against comparable stock.

DATA CLEANUP

What had to be fixed before modeling

Six columns arrived with '?' standing in for missing values — normalized losses, bore, stroke, horsepower, peak rpm, and number of doors. Numeric gaps were imputed with the column median, the door count with its mode, and every categorical field was then label- or one-hot-encoded before scaling.

FINDINGS & CONCLUSION

What the dashboard is actually telling us

  • RISK

    Rotor engines carry the highest insurance risk rating (symboling) of any engine type in the fleet.

  • SUPPLY

    Toyota builds more gas-powered cars than any other make, and leads in both two-door and four-door body counts.

  • PERFORMANCE

    Jaguar produces the single most powerful car in the dataset, at 262 hp — well above the fleet mean of roughly 104 hp.

  • PRICE

    Convertibles carry the highest average price of any body style, despite being the rarest — just 5 of the 200 cars.

  • TRADE-OFF

    City and highway mpg move almost in lockstep (r=0.97); horsepower buys speed at the direct expense of both (r≈−0.82).

  • TUNING

    CV-based hyperparameter search paid off for XGBoost (+0.13 R²) but backfired for Random Forest and KNN — a reminder that a better cross-validation score doesn't always mean a better holdout score.

  • RESULT

    The tuned Decision Tree and tuned MLP both land at R²≈0.931, tied for best. The notebook's call: Decision Tree is the more practical pick — same accuracy, far less training and inference overhead than a neural net.
